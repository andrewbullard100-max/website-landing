Nursing Home Kitchen Survey Readiness Checklist

By Andrew Bullard · October 28, 2026

A CMS surveyor reviewing the dietary department of a skilled nursing facility is looking for two things: evidence that the kitchen is operating safely on the day of the survey, and evidence that it has been operating safely consistently -- meaning the records they find should show a pattern of monitoring, not a flurry of activity that started when someone saw the survey team walk through the door.

This checklist is organized around the areas CMS surveyors assess under 42 CFR 483.60 (Food and Nutrition Services) and the associated F-tags (F800 through F812). It covers the food safety requirements, therapeutic diet accuracy, allergen control, documentation, and staff competency areas where kitchens most often receive citations.

Temperature control and food storage

The core food safety requirements in a nursing home kitchen are the same as in any food service operation, but the consequences of a failure are more serious. Most temperature and storage citations fall under F812, which applies the FDA Food Code standards to the nursing home context.

Temperature and storage checklist F812

Walk-in and reach-in refrigerators are holding at or below 41°F. Temperature logs show readings were taken at least once per shift, with corrective actions documented for any out-of-range readings.
Hot-held TCS foods are at or above 135°F at the time of service. Hot-holding logs show readings were taken and documented.
All prepared foods in refrigeration have date labels showing the preparation date and the use-by date. No items are past their 7-day maximum hold time.
Raw proteins are stored below ready-to-eat foods in the correct order: RTE foods on top, seafood, whole-cut meats, ground meats, poultry on the bottom.
Cooling records are available for foods that required active cooling. Cooked items are cooled from 135°F to 70°F within 2 hours and from 70°F to 41°F within the next 4 hours, with temperature readings documented at each stage.
Dry storage is organized with foods at least 6 inches off the floor. No evidence of moisture intrusion, pest activity, or damaged packaging.

Therapeutic diets and texture-modified foods

Therapeutic diet accuracy is one of the areas where nursing home kitchen citations diverge from standard restaurant inspections. A CMS surveyor reviewing dietary compliance will trace a resident's diet order from the physician's order in the medical record through to the care plan, the diet card or tray ticket, and the food that is actually prepared and served. Any break in that chain is a citation.

The most common gaps are: a diet order that was updated in the medical record but not reflected on the diet card, a texture-modified food that was prepared at a different consistency than the care plan specifies, or a staff member who cannot identify a resident's texture requirement without looking it up multiple times.

Therapeutic diet checklist F806

A current diet card or tray ticket is available for every resident. The diet listed on the card matches the current diet order in the medical record and the care plan.
Texture-modified diets specify the exact texture level (minced, finely minced, pureed) and thickening level for liquids, using consistent terminology across the medical record, care plan, and kitchen production records.
Staff working the tray line can identify the texture requirement for each resident being served without having to consult multiple documents. Texture training is documented in staff records.
Pureed foods are prepared to a consistent texture each time they appear on the menu. There is a written recipe or production standard that describes the preparation method and expected final texture.
A process is in place to communicate diet order changes from the nursing unit to the dietary department promptly -- same day or sooner for changes that affect the next meal service.

Allergen control

Allergen management in long-term care differs from a restaurant in one critical way: the resident may not be able to identify their own tray as incorrect. A resident with dementia, limited verbal communication, or difficulty reading cannot reliably serve as a check on whether the food in front of them matches their documented restrictions. The kitchen and tray line staff are the last verification point before service.

Allergen control checklist F812

Each resident's known food allergies and intolerances are documented in the medical record and reflected on the diet card or tray ticket. Allergy information is visible to staff preparing and assembling the tray.
When a resident has a documented allergy to a major food allergen, the kitchen has a procedure for substituting safe alternatives and for preventing cross-contact during preparation.
Staff can identify which menu items contain the eight major food allergens (milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, wheat, peanuts, soybeans, sesame). Ingredient information is available in the kitchen for each menu item.
A process is in place to update allergen documentation when a new allergy is identified or when a resident is admitted. Dietary is notified on the day of admission for any resident with a documented food allergy.

Sanitation and personal hygiene

Surveyors observe kitchen practices during meal preparation and service. Handwashing, glove use with ready-to-eat foods, and dish machine sanitation are among the most directly observable food safety practices -- and among the first things a surveyor will document if they are not being followed correctly.

Sanitation checklist F812

Handwashing sinks are stocked with soap and paper towels and are accessible to all staff. Staff are observed washing hands at required intervals -- after touching raw protein, after removing gloves, after using the restroom, after handling trash.
Staff are not making bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat foods. Gloves, tongs, or other utensils are used for all RTE food handling. Staff change gloves after any contamination event.
Dish machine sanitization is verified at the start of each meal service. Final rinse temperature (for high-temp machines) or chemical concentration (for low-temp machines) is documented in the dish machine log. Chemical test strips or a temperature-indicating device is available.
Sanitizer solution in buckets and spray bottles is at the correct concentration (50 to 200 ppm for chlorine; per manufacturer spec for quat). Concentration is verified with test strips each time a fresh solution is made and documented.
An employee health policy is in place and posted. Staff with symptoms of vomiting, diarrhea, jaundice, or skin sores that could contaminate food are excluded from food-contact duties and understand the policy.

Documentation and corrective action records

The documentation a kitchen team can produce during a survey tells a story about how the kitchen operates when no one is watching. A temperature log that shows readings taken every shift, with corrective actions documented whenever a reading was out of range, demonstrates an active monitoring system. A log with entries only on days when a surveyor was expected tells a different story.

Documentation checklist

Refrigeration temperature logs are current and cover at least the past 30 days. Out-of-range readings have documented corrective actions -- the action taken, who took it, and the result when the temperature was re-checked.
HACCP records are maintained and available. Critical control point monitoring logs show consistent checking at the required frequency.
Dish machine logs cover the past 30 days with a record of the sanitization verification at each meal service. Any days where the machine failed verification show what was done in response (manual sanitation, service call, etc.).
Corrective action records are maintained for food safety issues identified during internal audits or self-assessments. Each record shows the finding, the action taken, who was responsible, and the verification that the action was completed.
Staff training records show current food safety certification for the dietary manager or food service supervisor. Other dietary staff have documented food handler training consistent with the facility's training program.

Putting the checklist to work between surveys

The purpose of a survey readiness checklist is not to perform well when a surveyor arrives -- it is to run the kitchen the same way every day so that a surveyor arriving on any day finds the same operation. The kitchens that consistently avoid F-tag citations are not the ones that have a surge of activity when they see the survey team. They are the ones with a documentation system that captures monitoring data shift by shift, and a corrective action process that treats every flagged item as something that needs to be closed -- not just acknowledged.

For dietary directors overseeing multiple nursing homes or assisted living communities, the challenge is consistency across facilities. A kitchen that is well-managed at the flagship community but inconsistently monitored at a smaller satellite location carries risk for the whole system. The goal is a standard that applies uniformly, with documentation that shows each location is following it.
