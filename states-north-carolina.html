North Carolina Food Code: Restaurant Inspection Requirements

How NC DHHS inspects restaurants under 15A NCAC 18A .2600, what A, B, and C grade cards mean, how inspection frequency is set by risk category, and which violations inspectors cite most often. Everything you need to prepare for an unannounced inspection.

How North Carolina Food Inspections Work

Food service establishment inspections in North Carolina are conducted by local environmental health specialists employed by county health departments, operating under the authority of the North Carolina Department of Health and Human Services (NCDHHS). The program is governed by 15A NCAC 18A .2600, which adopts the FDA Food Code 2017 by reference and adds state-specific provisions.

Inspections are unannounced. Inspection frequency is set by risk category: Risk Category I facilities (lowest risk) are inspected at least once per year; Risk Category II facilities at least twice per year; and Risk Category III facilities (highest risk) at least three times per year. Risk category depends on the type of food the establishment handles, the preparation methods used, and the population served. Facilities that serve vulnerable populations such as nursing home and senior living dining programs are typically classified at higher risk categories.

Inspectors score the inspection on a 100-point scale. Each violation is assigned a point value based on its severity. The resulting score determines which grade card is issued and what follow-up action is required. Grade cards must be posted in a location visible to the public.

Risk Category and Inspection Frequency

Unlike states with a flat annual inspection schedule, North Carolina uses a three-tier risk-category system to set how often each establishment is inspected. The category is assigned by the local health department based on the establishment's operations.

Risk Category Minimum Inspections per Year Typical Establishment Types
Category I 1 Establishments handling only pre-packaged or minimally processed foods; limited menu operations with no complex food preparation
Category II 2 Establishments with moderate food preparation and handling of some potentially hazardous foods; most full-service restaurants
Category III 3 Establishments with extensive raw protein handling, complex preparation, large-volume food production, or service to vulnerable populations such as hospitals, nursing homes, and school cafeterias

The local environmental health specialist assigns the risk category during the permitting process. If an establishment changes its menu or service type significantly, the category may be reassigned. Operators can request a review of their risk classification through their county health department.

North Carolina's Grade Card System

North Carolina uses a letter-grade system based on 15A NCAC 18A .2662. Scores are on a 100-point scale, with points deducted for each violation. The resulting grade card must be posted where customers can see it. Scores below 70 trigger permit revocation proceedings under G.S. 130A-23(d).

A

Score: 90 to 100
The establishment is in substantial compliance with the North Carolina food code. Grade A indicates a high level of food safety practice. The grade card must be posted in a location visible to the public, including the entrance area.

B

Score: 80 to 89.5
The establishment has identified violations that need correction. A Grade B triggers a follow-up to verify corrections were completed. The grade card must still be posted and the establishment remains open pending correction of violations.

C

Score: 70 to 79.5
Significant violations are present. A Grade C triggers a re-inspection, typically within 30 days. All violations must be corrected before or during the re-inspection. The grade card must be posted. Continued low scores increase enforcement pressure.

Below 70 -- Revocation

Score: Below 70
A score below 70 initiates permit revocation proceedings under G.S. 130A-23(d). NCDHHS may also order immediate closure if an imminent health hazard is found at any score. A passing re-inspection is required before the establishment may reopen.

Governing Code and State-Specific Rules

North Carolina enforces the FDA Food Code 2017 by reference under 15A NCAC 18A .2600. The 2017 edition introduced several changes from prior versions, including updates to temperature requirements for certain foods, clarifications around employee health reporting, and revised language on variance procedures for specialized food processes.

Local environmental health specialists have authority to issue a Grade A, B, or C card and to initiate permit revocation proceedings under the state statute. They can also order immediate closure of an establishment if an imminent health hazard is identified -- defined as any condition that poses an immediate threat of serious injury or illness. Common imminent-hazard conditions include extended power outages affecting refrigeration, sewage backups, and serious vermin infestations.

Certified food protection manager (CFPM) requirement: Under the FDA Food Code 2017 (adopted by NC), establishments must have at least one employee who has supervisory and management responsibility and holds a valid CFPM certification from an ANSI-accredited program such as ServSafe or NRFSP. The certificate must be on-site and available for inspection. Failure to produce a valid certificate is cited as a violation.

Most Common Violations in North Carolina Restaurant Inspections

These are the categories where NC environmental health specialists most frequently find violations. Citations reference 15A NCAC 18A .2600, which adopts the FDA Food Code 2017. Most are preventable with consistent staff training, daily temperature logging, and documented corrective actions.

01
Improper temperature control for TCS foodsCold-held foods above 41°F or hot-held foods below 135°F. 15A NCAC 18A .2600 (FDA Food Code 2017 §§3-501.16(A)(1) and (A)(2)). Overfilled reach-ins, walk-in doors left open, and equipment that needs servicing are common causes. A documented temperature log showing readings every shift is the strongest defense during an inspection.
02
Handwashing violations and bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat foodsEmployees not washing hands at required intervals or making bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat (RTE) foods without an approved alternative such as gloves. 15A NCAC 18A .2600 (FDA Food Code 2017 §§2-301.12, 2-301.14, 3-301.11). These are priority violations that carry significant point deductions and can drop a score from A to C in a single citation.
03
Cross-contamination through improper storage orderRaw proteins stored above or adjacent to RTE foods. 15A NCAC 18A .2600 (FDA Food Code 2017 §3-302.11). The required storage order from top to bottom: RTE foods, seafood, whole intact meats, ground meats, poultry. Storing ground beef above lettuce is one of the most commonly cited violations in walk-in cooler inspections.
04
Date labeling missing or expiredPrepared foods without a use-by date or held past the 7-day maximum at 41°F or below. 15A NCAC 18A .2600 (FDA Food Code 2017 §§3-501.17, 3-501.18). Inspectors check date labels on every container in the cooler. A single expired item is a priority citation; multiple items suggest a systemic labeling problem that results in significant point deductions.
05
Sanitizer concentration out of specified rangeChlorine sanitizer below 50 ppm or above 200 ppm, or quat sanitizer outside the manufacturer range. 15A NCAC 18A .2600 (FDA Food Code 2017 §4-501.114). Test strips should be used every time a sanitizer bucket is made up. Keeping a log of sanitizer checks each shift demonstrates due diligence during inspections.
06
Pest evidence or structural deficienciesRodent or insect evidence in food storage or prep areas, or gaps in exterior walls that allow pest entry. 15A NCAC 18A .2600 (FDA Food Code 2017 §§6-501.111, 6-202.15). An active pest infestation can trigger an imminent health hazard declaration and immediate closure regardless of the overall score. Preventive pest management records should be kept on site.

How to Find North Carolina Restaurant Inspection Records

North Carolina restaurant inspection scores are public records. They are available through the NC DHHS Food and Drug Protection Division online portal and through individual county health department websites. The statewide portal allows searching by establishment name, permit number, county, or address, and shows the most recent inspection score and grade card issued.

Inspection reports include the overall score, the letter grade issued, and a line-by-line account of every violation found with the applicable code section and the point deduction. Historical inspections are also available, which lets the public and operators review a facility's compliance trend over time.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often are restaurants inspected in North Carolina?
Inspection frequency is risk-based. Risk Category I establishments are inspected at least once per year. Risk Category II establishments are inspected at least twice per year. Risk Category III establishments, such as operations handling large volumes of raw proteins or serving vulnerable populations, are inspected at least three times per year. All routine inspections are unannounced and conducted by local county environmental health specialists under NCDHHS authority.
What do the A, B, and C grade cards mean in North Carolina?
Grade A (90 to 100) means substantial compliance. Grade B (80 to 89.5) indicates violations requiring follow-up. Grade C (70 to 79.5) indicates significant violations and triggers a re-inspection, typically within 30 days. A score below 70 initiates permit revocation proceedings under G.S. 130A-23(d). The grade card must be posted in a location visible to the public at all times.
What regulation governs food safety inspections in North Carolina?
North Carolina food establishment inspections are governed by 15A NCAC 18A .2600, which adopts the FDA Food Code 2017 by reference. Inspections are conducted by local environmental health specialists employed by county health departments, operating under the authority of the NC Department of Health and Human Services.
What happens if a North Carolina restaurant scores below 70?
A score below 70 triggers permit revocation proceedings under G.S. 130A-23(d). The establishment may also be ordered to close immediately if any imminent health hazard is identified, regardless of the overall score. A passing re-inspection is required before the establishment may reopen after a closure or permit suspension.
