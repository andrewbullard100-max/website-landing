Georgia Restaurant Inspections: Grades, Violations and Compliance

How Georgia's county Environmental Health Specialists inspect food establishments under Chapter 511-6-1, what the A through U letter grades mean, which violations get cited most, and what CFPM requirements apply statewide.

How Georgia Food Inspections Work

Food safety inspections in Georgia are conducted by Environmental Health Specialists employed by county health departments and overseen by the Georgia Department of Public Health (DPH). The governing rule is Chapter 511-6-1 of the Georgia Rules and Regulations for Food Service, based on the FDA Food Code.

Most Georgia restaurants receive at least two inspections per year -- one routine inspection and at least one follow-up -- with higher-risk facilities, complaint-based inspections, and facilities with a history of violations receiving additional visits. All routine inspections are unannounced.

Georgia is one of the few states where both the letter grade and the numeric score must be posted near the entrance in a location visible to customers. The grade card is approximately 8.5" x 11" and must remain posted until the next inspection. Removing or obscuring the grade card is a violation.

The label "Food Safety Excellence" is used by Georgia DPH to recognize establishments that consistently score in the A range. This distinction can appear on inspection reports and is used by some counties in consumer-facing materials.

Quick Reference

Regulatory authority: Georgia Department of Public Health (DPH)

Local enforcement: County Environmental Health departments

Governing rule: Chapter 511-6-1, Georgia Rules and Regulations for Food Service

Adopted food code: Based on FDA Food Code (confirm current edition with Georgia DPH)

Inspection frequency: At least twice per year; more for high-risk or non-compliant facilities

Inspections announced: No -- routine inspections are unannounced

Grade posting requirement: Letter grade and numeric score must be posted near the entrance

Excellence designation: "Food Safety Excellence" for consistent A-range scores

TCS cold holding: 41°F or below

TCS hot holding: 135°F or above

Georgia's Letter Grading System

Georgia uses a 100-point scoring system with four possible grades. The letter grade and numeric score are both posted at the entrance. A "U" grade -- Unsatisfactory -- signals serious compliance failures and triggers immediate action.

A

Score: 90 to 100 (Food Safety Excellence)
Substantial compliance with Chapter 511-6-1. The "Food Safety Excellence" designation may accompany A grades from facilities with a consistent compliance history. Posted at the entrance alongside the numeric score.

B

Score: 80 to 89
Compliance issues present. A B grade triggers a mandatory follow-up inspection. The B card is posted at the entrance. Violations must be corrected before the follow-up, and the follow-up result becomes the record for that inspection cycle.

C

Score: 70 to 79
Significant compliance failures. A C triggers immediate re-inspection. The C card is posted prominently at the entrance. Repeated C grades substantially increase the frequency of future inspections and the risk of permit action.

U

Score: below 70 (Unsatisfactory)
Immediate enforcement action. A U grade indicates conditions Georgia DPH considers unsafe for public food service. The facility may be required to close immediately and cannot reopen until a re-inspection confirms corrections. A U card posted at the entrance is highly visible to customers.

Certified Food Protection Manager (CFPM) Requirements

Georgia's Chapter 511-6-1 requires that food establishments have a Person in Charge (PIC) on premises during all hours of operation who demonstrates food safety knowledge. A Certified Food Protection Manager (CFPM) -- someone who has passed an accredited exam such as ServSafe, NRFSP (National Registry of Food Safety Professionals), or Prometric -- satisfies the knowledge requirement under the rule.

The CFPM certificate must be from an accreditation body recognized by the American National Standards Institute (ANSI). Operators should verify the specific number of required managers and any exam renewal requirements directly with their county Environmental Health department, as local enforcement details may vary.

Common CFPM exam providers recognized in Georgia include:

  • ServSafe (National Restaurant Association)
  • NRFSP (National Registry of Food Safety Professionals)
  • Prometric (Certified Professional Food Manager)
  • 360training (Learn2Serve)

Certificates are typically valid for five years. Operators should keep a copy of each CFPM certificate on file at the facility for inspector review.

Most Common Violations in Georgia Restaurant Inspections

These are the categories where Georgia's Environmental Health Specialists most frequently cite violations under Chapter 511-6-1. Each one is preventable with consistent daily practice.

01
Temperature control failuresCold TCS foods held above 41°F or hot TCS foods below 135°F. This is the highest-frequency critical violation category in Georgia and one of the most heavily weighted items on the inspection form. Walk-in cooler temperatures should be documented at least twice per shift.
02
Improper coolingGeorgia follows the FDA Food Code two-stage cooling requirement: cooked food must cool from 135°F to 70°F within 2 hours, then from 70°F to 41°F within an additional 4 hours. Large pots of soup, braised meats, and starches are the most common problem items. Covered hot containers placed directly in the walk-in routinely fail this standard.
03
Food storage order in coolersRaw proteins stored above ready-to-eat foods. Georgia inspectors check refrigerator and walk-in storage organization on every visit. The correct top-to-bottom storage order under Chapter 511-6-1 is: ready-to-eat foods, seafood, whole-cut meats, ground meats, poultry.
04
Employee hygiene and handwashingFailure to wash hands at required points, bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat foods, or working while ill. Georgia's Chapter 511-6-1 specifically addresses employee health reporting requirements -- operators must have a written employee health policy on file.
05
Date marking failuresReady-to-eat TCS foods prepared on site and held more than 24 hours must be dated and discarded within 7 days. Georgia inspectors check every container in the cooler for labels. Unlabeled containers are one of the fastest ways to lose points on an inspection.
06
Warewashing and sanitizer complianceEquipment and utensils washed but not sanitized, or sanitizer concentration out of range. Georgia inspectors test sanitizer with test strips and may check dish machine rinse temperatures with a thermometer. Both are quick checks that reveal recurring process failures.

How to Find Georgia Inspection Records

Georgia restaurant inspection reports are maintained at the county Environmental Health department level. The Georgia DPH Environmental Health page links to county health department contacts. Some counties publish inspection results online; others require a direct request to the county office.

To look up a specific restaurant's inspection history, identify the county where the establishment operates, then contact that county's Environmental Health department. Many county health departments in Georgia now offer online inspection databases -- check your county's health department website for an available search tool.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often are Georgia restaurants inspected?
Most Georgia food establishments receive at least two routine inspections per year under Chapter 511-6-1. Higher-risk facilities, establishments with a history of violations, and those that receive complaints may be inspected more frequently. All routine inspections are unannounced.
What does a U grade mean for a Georgia restaurant?
A U (Unsatisfactory) grade is issued when a restaurant scores below 70 on the 100-point inspection. It signals conditions Georgia DPH considers unsafe for public food service. The U card must be posted at the entrance, and the facility may be required to close immediately until a re-inspection confirms corrections.
Does Georgia require a Certified Food Protection Manager?
Yes. Georgia's Chapter 511-6-1 requires a Person in Charge who demonstrates food safety knowledge. A Certified Food Protection Manager (CFPM) certified through an ANSI-accredited program such as ServSafe or NRFSP satisfies this requirement. Confirm current requirements with your county Environmental Health department.
Where can I find Georgia restaurant inspection records?
Inspection reports are maintained by county Environmental Health departments. The Georgia DPH food service page links to county contacts. Some counties publish results online; contact your county health department directly for the most current records.
