FIFO Isn't Just a Warehouse Term — It's a Food Safety Control

First in, first out and date labeling aren't inventory housekeeping. They're how a kitchen actually knows what's still safe to serve, instead of guessing.

"First in, first out" gets talked about as an inventory discipline — don't waste product, don't let anything go stale in the back of a shelf. That's true, but it undersells what FIFO is actually for in a food safety context: it's the mechanism that keeps a kitchen's storage areas from quietly accumulating product that's no longer safe to serve, hidden behind everything that was shelved more recently.

What FIFO actually means in practice

The rule is simple to state: older product gets used before newer product of the same item, and that only works if newer deliveries get shelved behind or below what's already there, not in front of it. The failure mode is almost always the opposite — a new case of something gets set on the shelf nearest the door because that's the fastest place to put it down during a busy receiving window, while the older case sits further back, gets overlooked, and ages past its safe window without anyone registering it.

Why date labeling is the other half of this

FIFO only works if there's a date to rotate by. For ready-to-eat, temperature-controlled food that's been prepped in-house, the standard practice under the FDA Food Code is to mark it with the date it was prepared and treat it as needing to be used, sold, or discarded within 7 days when held at 41°F or below — a rule built around how long certain bacteria that can survive refrigeration, like Listeria, take to grow to a level that matters, even at safe cooler temperatures. Past that window, the food can still look and smell completely normal while no longer being considered safe to serve.

That's the part that makes date labeling non-optional rather than a nice-to-have: there's no sensory test that reliably catches this kind of risk. The label is the only record.

Where this actually goes wrong: it's rarely a kitchen with no labeling system at all. It's a kitchen where labeling happens inconsistently — one prep cook dates everything, another forgets during a rush — so some containers in the walk-in have a reliable date and others don't, and nobody can tell which is which at a glance.

Where this breaks down in a real kitchen

  • New product shelved in front of or above older product, simply because it's faster during a busy delivery window.
  • Inconsistent labeling — some prep gets dated, some doesn't, depending on who's working and how busy they are.
  • Expired product discovered during a deep clean rather than a routine check — which means it sat there, unnoticed, for however long it took to get to that cleaning task.
  • Date labels that record the wrong date — the day it was portioned rather than the day it was originally prepared, which resets a clock that was never supposed to reset.

What a working system looks like

Three things make FIFO and date labeling actually hold up: a labeling tool (dot stickers, a dating gun, a consistent written format) that's always within reach at every prep station, not stored somewhere that makes it easier to skip; a storage layout where the "old stock goes here, new stock goes there" rule is physically obvious rather than something to remember; and a routine spot-check — someone actually looking at dates in the walk-in on a schedule, not waiting to find an expired container by accident.

Why this matters more as an operation grows

A single kitchen with one prep team can keep this consistent through habit. Add more locations, more prep staff, and more suppliers, and the chance that labeling discipline holds everywhere, every shift, drops fast — which is exactly why a documented, verified system matters more than good intentions once an operation is bigger than one room anyone can personally keep an eye on.
