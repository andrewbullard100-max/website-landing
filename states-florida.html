Florida Restaurant Inspections: DBPR Compliance Guide

How Florida's Division of Hotels and Restaurants within DBPR inspects food establishments at least twice a year, how the three violation categories work, why Florida doesn't use letter grades, and which violations are most likely to result in a warning or closure order.

How Florida Food Inspections Work

Food safety inspections in Florida are conducted by the Division of Hotels and Restaurants within the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR). Florida does not use a letter grade system. Instead, DBPR assigns violations to three categories and tracks them on a standardized inspection form derived from the FDA Food Code, as adopted through Rule 61C-1.001, Florida Administrative Code.

Florida law requires that food service establishments be inspected at least twice per year. Facilities with a history of violations, those handling highly hazardous foods, or those receiving complaints may be inspected more frequently. All routine inspections are unannounced.

After an inspection, the establishment has the right to receive a copy of the inspection report. Florida law also requires that restaurants make their most recent inspection report available to any member of the public who asks for it. Inspection results are also published on DBPR's website and searchable by the public.

A key feature of Florida's system is that inspectors can issue an Emergency Order for Suspension of License when they find conditions that represent an immediate public health hazard. This order closes the facility on the spot, and the operator must correct all conditions and pass a re-inspection before reopening.

Quick Reference

Regulatory authority: Florida DBPR, Division of Hotels and Restaurants

Governing rules: Chapter 509, Florida Statutes; Food Rule 61C-1.001 (adopting FDA Food Code 2017)

Grading system: No letter grades -- three violation category tiers

Violation categories: High Priority, Intermediate, Basic

Inspection frequency: At least twice per year; more for high-risk or complaint facilities

Inspections announced: No -- routine inspections are unannounced

Inspection records: Must be made available to the public on request; also searchable at DBPR's website

Emergency closure: Emergency Order for Suspension of License for immediate health hazards

TCS cold holding: 41°F or below

TCS hot holding: 135°F or above

How Florida Categorizes Violations

Florida uses three violation categories under DBPR's inspection system. The category assigned to a violation determines how urgently it must be corrected and how DBPR treats repeat findings across inspection cycles.

High Priority

The most serious violations, directly linked to conditions that can cause foodborne illness: improper temperatures for TCS foods, inadequate cooking, bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat foods, employee illness, pest evidence, and improper chemical storage near food. High Priority violations must be corrected immediately when possible. DBPR tracks them across inspection cycles -- repeat High Priority findings accelerate enforcement.

Intermediate

Violations related to general food protection practices, sanitation of equipment and utensils, food source documentation, and required employee certifications. Intermediate violations are less immediately dangerous than High Priority but reflect systemic compliance weaknesses. A missing or expired food manager certification is an Intermediate violation. These are corrected within a required timeframe set by the inspector.

Basic

Violations related to facility maintenance, equipment condition, lighting, ventilation, and general housekeeping. Basic violations do not pose an immediate health risk but signal underlying operational issues. Repeated Basic violations in the same category often indicate a training or maintenance gap that will appear on future inspections as well.

Most Common Violations in Florida Restaurant Inspections

DBPR inspectors evaluate all inspection points on every routine visit. These are the categories where violations appear most frequently across Florida's food service establishments.

01
TCS food temperature control (High Priority)Cold-held foods above 41°F or hot-held foods below 135°F. Florida's heat and humidity mean refrigeration equipment works harder and temperatures can drift more quickly than in cooler climates. Calibrated thermometers and twice-per-shift temperature logs are the minimum standard.
02
Handwashing and bare-hand contact (High Priority)Failure to wash hands at required points or making bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat foods. DBPR inspectors observe employee behavior during the inspection. Florida's high-volume tourism-driven restaurant environment creates pressure that sometimes leads staff to skip handwashing steps.
03
Improper cooling (High Priority)Hot food not cooled from 135°F to 70°F within 2 hours, or from 70°F to 41°F within the following 4 hours. In high-volume kitchens, large pots of sauces, soups, and proteins are the most common problem. Covered, hot containers placed directly in coolers are the most frequent cause of cooling violations.
04
Food storage and cross-contamination (High Priority)Raw proteins stored above ready-to-eat foods, or foods stored without protection from contamination. DBPR inspectors check cooler organization on every visit. Florida's seafood-heavy menus mean raw shellfish, finfish, and proteins from multiple species are frequently stored in close proximity.
05
Pest evidence (High Priority)Roach activity, rodent droppings, or flying insects in food prep or storage areas. Florida's climate supports year-round pest pressure, making pest control documentation and regular prevention treatments a compliance requirement. Evidence of pests during inspection can result in immediate closure.
06
Certified food manager on duty (Intermediate)Florida requires that a certified food manager be available during operating hours under Food Rule 61C-4. If the person on duty cannot produce a valid ANSI-accredited certification, it is an Intermediate violation. With multiple shifts and high turnover in Florida's hospitality industry, tracking which managers are certified and scheduling them appropriately requires active management.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often does DBPR inspect Florida restaurants?
Florida law requires food service establishments to be inspected at least twice per year. Facilities with a history of violations, those handling highly hazardous foods, or those receiving complaints may be inspected more frequently. All routine inspections are unannounced.
Does Florida use letter grades for restaurant inspections?
No. Florida does not use a letter-grade system. DBPR assigns violations to three categories (High Priority, Intermediate, and Basic) and tracks them across inspection cycles. Restaurants are required to make their most recent inspection report available to any member of the public who requests it, and results are also publicly searchable on the DBPR website.
What is a High Priority violation in Florida?
High Priority violations are the most serious violations in Florida's DBPR system -- violations most directly linked to conditions that can cause foodborne illness: improper temperatures for TCS foods, inadequate cooking, bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat foods, employee illness, pest evidence, and improper chemical storage near food. They must be corrected immediately when possible.
Does Florida require a Certified Food Manager?
Yes. Florida requires that a certified food manager be available during operating hours under Food Rule 61C-4. The manager must hold a valid ANSI-accredited food manager certification. If the person on duty cannot produce a valid certification during an inspection, it is an Intermediate violation. Confirm current requirements with DBPR directly.
