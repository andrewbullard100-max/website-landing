Florida Food Code: A Complete Guide for Restaurant Operators

How Florida's DBPR inspects restaurants at least twice a year, what the 53-point checklist covers, why Florida doesn't use letter grades, and which violations are most likely to result in a warning or closure order.

How Florida Food Inspections Work

Food safety inspections in Florida are conducted by the Division of Hotels and Restaurants within the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR). Florida does not use a letter grade system. Instead, DBPR assigns violations to categories and tracks them on a 53-point inspection checklist derived from the FDA Food Code.

Florida law requires that food service establishments be inspected at least twice per year. Facilities with a history of violations, those handling highly hazardous foods, or those receiving complaints may be inspected more frequently. All routine inspections are unannounced.

After an inspection, the establishment has the right to receive a copy of the inspection report. Florida law also requires that restaurants make their most recent inspection report available to any member of the public who asks for it. Inspection results are also published on DBPR's website and searchable by the public.

A key feature of Florida's system is that inspectors can issue an Emergency Order for Suspension of License when they find conditions that represent an immediate public health hazard. This order closes the facility on the spot, and the operator must correct all conditions and pass a re-inspection before reopening.

Quick Reference

Regulatory authority: Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR), Division of Hotels and Restaurants

Grading system: No letter grades -- pass/fail with violation categories

Inspection form: 53-point checklist

Inspection frequency: At least twice per year; more for high-risk or complaint facilities

Inspections announced: No -- routine inspections are unannounced

Inspection records: Must be made available to the public on request; also searchable at DBPR's website

Emergency closure: Emergency Order for Suspension of License when immediate health hazard exists

TCS cold holding: 41°F or below

TCS hot holding: 135°F or above

How Florida Categorizes Violations

Florida doesn't use letter grades, but it does categorize violations by severity. The distinction between High Priority and Basic violations determines how quickly you must correct them and how inspectors treat repeat findings.

High Priority Violations
These are the violations most directly linked to conditions that can cause foodborne illness: improper temperatures for TCS foods, improper cooking, bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat foods, employee illness, improper chemical storage near food, and pest evidence. High Priority violations must be corrected immediately when possible, and DBPR tracks them across inspection cycles to identify chronic problems.
Basic Violations
These violations are less immediately dangerous but reflect systemic compliance problems: improper equipment condition, missing certifications, inadequate lighting or ventilation, and documentation gaps. Basic violations are still recorded and can accumulate to a pattern that increases inspection frequency. Repeated basic violations in the same category often signal an underlying training or management issue.

Most Common Violations in Florida Restaurant Inspections

DBPR inspectors cover all 53 checklist points on every routine visit. These are the categories where violations appear most frequently across Florida's food service establishments.

01
TCS food temperature controlCold-held foods above 41°F or hot-held foods below 135°F are High Priority violations. Florida's heat and humidity mean refrigeration equipment works harder and temperatures can drift more quickly than in cooler climates. Calibrated thermometers and twice-per-shift temperature logs are the minimum standard.
02
Handwashing and bare-hand contactFailure to wash hands at required points or making bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat foods. DBPR inspectors observe employee behavior during the inspection. Florida's high-volume tourism-driven restaurant environment creates pressure that sometimes leads staff to skip handwashing steps.
03
Improper coolingHot food not cooled from 135°F to 70°F within 2 hours, or from 70°F to 41°F within the following 4 hours. In high-volume kitchens, large pots of sauces, soups, and proteins are the most common problem. Covered, hot containers placed directly in coolers are the most frequent cause of cooling violations.
04
Food storage and cross-contaminationRaw proteins stored above ready-to-eat foods, or foods stored without protection from contamination. DBPR inspectors check cooler organization on every visit. Florida's seafood-heavy menus mean raw shellfish, finfish, and proteins from multiple species are frequently stored in close proximity.
05
Pest evidenceRoach activity, rodent droppings, or flying insects in food prep or storage areas. Florida's climate supports year-round pest pressure, making pest control documentation and regular prevention treatments a compliance requirement, not just a preference. Evidence of pests during inspection can result in immediate closure.
06
Certified food manager on dutyFlorida requires that a certified food manager be available during operating hours. If the person on duty cannot produce a valid ANSI-accredited certification, it is a violation. With multiple shifts and high turnover in Florida's hospitality industry, tracking which managers are certified and scheduling them appropriately requires active management.
