South Carolina restaurant inspections: SCDA letter grades and compliance

South Carolina's inspection authority changed on July 1, 2024 when SCDA took over from DHEC. Here is how inspections work now under Regulation 61-25, what A through C grades mean, when a permit can be revoked, and what violations are most commonly cited.

Enforcement authority changed July 1, 2024 As of July 1, 2024, the South Carolina Department of Agriculture (SCDA) handles all retail food establishment inspections in South Carolina. This responsibility previously rested with DHEC. If your compliance records or contacts reference DHEC for restaurant inspections, update them -- the correct authority is now SCDA.

How South Carolina food inspections work

Food safety inspections in South Carolina are conducted by the South Carolina Department of Agriculture (SCDA). The governing regulation is S.C. Regulation 61-25 (Retail Food Establishments), which is based on an FDA Food Code 2017 framework.

South Carolina uses a risk-based inspection system, meaning the frequency of routine inspections depends on the risk category of the food operation. Facilities handling raw proteins, cooking to order, serving vulnerable populations, or operating with a history of violations are inspected more frequently than lower-risk operations such as convenience stores that sell only pre-packaged food.

All routine inspections are unannounced. The inspection produces a percentage score, and the resulting score determines the letter grade posted. Grade cards must be prominently posted and are visible to customers at the establishment.

Inspection results are publicly available through scfoodgrades.com, where consumers can look up any licensed food service establishment in the state. This transparency creates a direct public accountability link between your grade and your customer-facing reputation.

South Carolina letter grading system

South Carolina uses a percentage-based scoring system with three passing grades. A score below 70 results in permit revocation. Results are posted publicly at scfoodgrades.com.

A

Score: 90 to 100
The facility is in substantial compliance with S.C. Regulation 61-25. An A grade signals a well-run operation and is the standard expectation. Grade is posted at the establishment and searchable on scfoodgrades.com.

B

Score: 80 to 89
Compliance gaps are present and must be corrected. A B grade triggers a follow-up inspection. The facility may remain open, but the B grade is posted publicly. Violations must be addressed before the follow-up visit.

C

Score: 70 to 79
Significant compliance failures. A C grade triggers immediate re-inspection and is posted publicly on scfoodgrades.com. Repeated C grades substantially increase inspection frequency and escalate regulatory scrutiny.

Revoked

Score: Below 70
SCDA may revoke the food service permit. The facility cannot operate legally until violations are corrected and a re-inspection confirms compliance. Permit revocation is a public record and appears in the SCDA enforcement history.

CFPM requirements in South Carolina

S.C. Regulation 61-25, based on the FDA Food Code 2017 framework, requires that food establishments have access to a person who can demonstrate food safety knowledge to an SCDA inspector. The Person in Charge during each operating shift must be able to demonstrate knowledge of safe food handling, foodborne illness risk factors, and proper corrective actions.

For operations that cook raw animal proteins to order, serve highly susceptible populations, or perform specialized food processes, the obligation to have a trained and knowledgeable person in charge is strictly applied during inspections. SCDA inspectors may question the Person in Charge directly about food safety practices and applicable code requirements.

Operators should confirm current Certified Food Protection Manager (CFPM) certification requirements directly with SCDA, as specific obligations and any exemptions for lower-risk operations are subject to regulatory interpretation and change. Multi-unit operators with locations in other states should ensure their compliance program accounts for any differences between South Carolina's requirements and those of their other operating states.

Most common violations in South Carolina restaurant inspections

These are the violation categories most frequently identified by SCDA inspectors under S.C. Regulation 61-25. Many are preventable with consistent daily routines and documented procedures.

01
Temperature control for TCS foodsCold-held foods found above 41°F or hot-held foods below 135°F are among the highest-weighted violation categories because they directly create conditions for bacterial growth. SCDA inspectors measure temperatures directly with calibrated thermometers during every routine visit.
02
Improper food storage and cross-contaminationRaw proteins stored above or adjacent to ready-to-eat foods. Inspectors check cooler and walk-in organization on every visit. Improperly stored raw poultry above ready-to-eat salads is one of the most common critical violations seen in South Carolina inspections under Regulation 61-25.
03
Handwashing and employee hygieneFailure to wash hands at required intervals, or employees observed making bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat foods. SCDA inspectors observe employee behavior during the visit. Handwashing sink accessibility is also checked -- a sink blocked by equipment or supplies is a violation.
04
Date labeling failuresReady-to-eat TCS foods prepared on site and held longer than 24 hours must be date-marked and discarded no later than 7 days after preparation. SCDA inspectors check every container in the walk-in. Missing or incorrect date labels on deli meats, prepared salads, and sauces are consistent findings.
05
Sanitizer concentration out of rangeChlorine-based sanitizers below 50 ppm or above 200 ppm, or quaternary ammonium solutions outside the manufacturer's required range. Inspectors test sanitizer solutions with test strips. Sanitizer buckets that are not refreshed during the shift are a common source of out-of-range readings.
06
Pest evidence and facility maintenanceEvidence of cockroaches, rodents, or flying insects in food preparation or storage areas. South Carolina's warm coastal climate creates year-round pest pressure, particularly in coastal counties. Pest control contracts and facility sealing documentation should be maintained and available for inspection.

Frequently asked questions

Who inspects restaurants in South Carolina now?

The South Carolina Department of Agriculture (SCDA) has inspected all retail food establishments in South Carolina since July 1, 2024. This responsibility was previously held by DHEC. If your compliance contacts or records reference DHEC for restaurant inspections, update them to SCDA.

What does a C grade mean for a South Carolina restaurant?

A C grade represents a score between 70 and 79 under SCDA Regulation 61-25. It indicates significant compliance failures and triggers an immediate re-inspection. The C grade is posted publicly at scfoodgrades.com and is visible to any customer who looks up the establishment.

Where can I see South Carolina restaurant inspection grades?

SCDA posts letter grades for all licensed food service establishments at scfoodgrades.com. Consumers can search by establishment name or location. The grade on the physical placard at the establishment and the grade on scfoodgrades.com should match.

How often are South Carolina restaurants inspected?

SCDA uses a risk-based inspection schedule under Regulation 61-25. Higher-risk establishments that cook raw proteins to order, serve vulnerable populations, or have a history of violations are inspected more frequently than lower-risk operations. All routine inspections are unannounced.
