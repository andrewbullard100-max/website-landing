Alabama restaurant inspections: scoring, violations, and compliance

Alabama uses a 100-point deduction-based system under Ala. Admin. Code r. 420-3-22. Here is what each score range means, how ADPH responds to violations, and what operators are most often cited for.

How Alabama food inspections work

Food safety inspections in Alabama are conducted by the Alabama Department of Public Health (ADPH), Environmental Health Services. County-level Environmental Health Officers carry out inspections statewide under state-level oversight and standardization.

Alabama follows a 100-point deduction-based system under Ala. Admin. Code r. 420-3-22 (most recently revised February 14, 2025), which adopted the FDA Food Code 2013 with specific exclusions. Facilities start at 100 and lose points for each violation found. The final score determines the status level and the required enforcement response.

Routine inspections are unannounced. Frequency is risk-based: high-volume restaurants that cook raw proteins to order are generally inspected at least twice per year; lower-risk operations are inspected less frequently.

Critical violations require immediate corrective action during the inspection whenever possible. If a critical violation cannot be corrected on-site, it triggers a follow-up re-inspection. A facility that scores below 60 faces immediate closure until violations are corrected and a passing re-inspection is completed.

Alabama inspection results are publicly searchable at foodscores.state.al.us, allowing any consumer to look up the most recent score for any licensed food service establishment in the state.

Quick reference

Regulatory authority: ADPH Environmental Health Services

Governing code: Ala. Admin. Code r. 420-3-22-.01(3), rev. Feb. 14, 2025 (FDA Food Code 2013 basis)

Alabama inspection scoring thresholds

Alabama starts every inspection at 100 points and deducts for violations found. Each threshold carries a specific enforcement response. Critical violations at any score level trigger a re-inspection regardless of total score.

Satisfactory

Score: 85 to 100
The facility is in substantial compliance. No mandatory score-based follow-up is required, though any critical violation noted during the inspection still triggers a re-inspection. Results posted at foodscores.state.al.us.

Follow-Up

Score: 70 to 84
The facility must complete corrective actions within 60 days. A follow-up inspection is scheduled. The establishment may remain open. Operators who do not resolve violations before the re-inspection risk a lower score at the follow-up visit.

Immediate Action

Score: 60 to 69
Significant compliance failures require corrective action within 48 hours. ADPH schedules an urgent re-inspection. The facility may remain open but is considered high-risk and subject to increased inspection frequency.

Closure

Score: Below 60
The facility is closed immediately. It cannot reopen until all violations are corrected and a passing re-inspection is completed. Closures are a matter of public record on foodscores.state.al.us.

Critical vs. non-critical violations in Alabama

Alabama's inspection form (ADPH-FLP-103) distinguishes between critical violations and non-critical violations, and the distinction matters for enforcement responses.

Critical violations are items directly linked to foodborne illness risk: improper holding temperatures, inadequate cooking temperatures, employee illness or poor hygiene, contamination from raw proteins, approved food sources, and chemical contamination. A critical violation found at any score level triggers a re-inspection, regardless of whether the total score clears the 85-point Satisfactory threshold. ADPH expects immediate corrective action on-site whenever possible.

Non-critical violations are items related to facility maintenance, equipment condition, labeling, and general sanitation practices. These carry deductions and contribute to the total score but do not independently trigger a re-inspection timeline when corrected promptly.

The ADPH-FLP-103 form has 38 scored line items. Each item is weighted by its risk level, so the total deduction from a single critical violation can be substantial. Operators should review the full form and understand which items are critical before their next inspection.

CFPM requirements in Alabama

Alabama adopted the FDA Food Code 2013 but excluded sections 2-102.11(A) and (C) from adoption. These are the provisions that require a Certified Food Protection Manager (CFPM) to be employed by food establishments with certain risk profiles.

As a result, Alabama does not require every food establishment to employ a nationally certified food protection manager (such as a ServSafe or Prometric certificate holder). The Person in Charge on duty must still be able to demonstrate food safety knowledge to an ADPH inspector under 2-102.11 generally, but the affirmative obligation to hold a third-party accredited certification does not apply statewide under Ala. Admin. Code r. 420-3-22.

Operators with locations in multiple states should verify: if your other states require a CFPM, you will need those certifications for those locations even if Alabama does not require one. Confirm current requirements with ADPH Environmental Health Services before assuming no CFPM is needed at a specific location type.

For healthcare and senior living dining operations, federal CMS regulations under 42 CFR 483.60 impose separate documentation and competency standards for dietary staff that apply regardless of state CFPM exemptions.

Most common violations in Alabama restaurant inspections

ADPH inspectors focus heavily on critical items that create immediate food safety risk. These are the violations most frequently cited in Alabama food service inspections under Ala. Admin. Code r. 420-3-22.

01
Cold holding above 41 degrees FTCS foods in reach-in or walk-in coolers found above 41°F are among the most commonly cited and most heavily weighted violations in Alabama inspections. Inspectors probe every cold-held TCS item. Overcrowded coolers, door seal failures, and excessive door opening during service are the most common causes. Cited under the temperature control for safety (TCS) provisions of r. 420-3-22.
02
Employee illness and hygiene failuresEmployees reporting for work while ill with vomiting, diarrhea, jaundice, or a diagnosed reportable illness are a critical violation. Inspectors also check for bare-hand contact with ready-to-eat foods and whether employees are washing hands correctly at required intervals throughout the shift.
03
Inadequate cooking temperaturesUndercooked poultry (must reach 165°F), ground beef (155°F), or other raw animal foods are critical violations. ADPH inspectors may test food temperatures directly or observe cooking processes. Failure to reach required minimum internal temperatures requires immediate corrective action.
04
Improper food storage and cross-contaminationRaw animal proteins stored above ready-to-eat foods in coolers or on shelves. ADPH inspectors check cooler organization on every visit. Improperly stored raw chicken above deli meats or produce is one of the most visible and heavily weighted violations found in Alabama kitchens.
05
Date marking and food labeling failuresReady-to-eat TCS foods prepared on site and held over 24 hours must carry a date label with a discard date no more than 7 days from preparation. Inspectors check every container in the walk-in. Missing labels, incorrect dates, or food held past the discard date are common findings in Alabama inspections.
06
Pest evidence and structural maintenanceEvidence of cockroaches, rodents, or flies in food storage or preparation areas is a critical violation in Alabama. The state's hot, humid climate creates year-round pest pressure. Active pest control contracts and facility sealing documentation should be maintained and available on request.

Frequently asked questions

What is a failing score in Alabama restaurant inspections?

A score below 60 on the 100-point ADPH scale triggers immediate closure. The facility cannot reopen until all violations are corrected and a passing re-inspection is completed. Scores between 60 and 69 require corrective action within 48 hours; scores between 70 and 84 require a follow-up inspection within 60 days.

How often are Alabama restaurants inspected?

Inspection frequency is risk-based under Ala. Admin. Code r. 420-3-22. High-risk operations that cook raw proteins to order are generally inspected at least twice per year. Lower-risk establishments such as those serving only pre-packaged foods are inspected less frequently. All routine inspections are unannounced.

Does Alabama require a Certified Food Protection Manager?

Alabama adopted the FDA Food Code 2013 but excluded sections 2-102.11(A) and (C), which are the provisions that require a CFPM for all food establishments. The Person in Charge must demonstrate food safety knowledge, but a nationally accredited CFPM certification is not mandated statewide. Confirm current requirements directly with ADPH Environmental Health Services.

Where can I look up Alabama restaurant inspection scores?

Alabama inspection results are publicly available at foodscores.state.al.us. Any consumer can search for a licensed food service establishment by name or location to see the most recent inspection score and findings.
